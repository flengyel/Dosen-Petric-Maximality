                 Maximality of the Došen–Petrić Boolean Category
                                                 Florian Lengyel∗

                                                October 28, 2026



                                                     Abstract
              The freely generated zero-identity Boolean category of Došen and Petrić is maximal: every
          additional equation between distinct parallel arrows, closed under the proof operations and
          uniform formula substitution, makes the quotient a preorder. This disproves the nonmaximality
          conjecture in Section 14.2 of the September 2007 revision of Proof-Theoretical Coherence. Boolean
          Coherence reduces a new equation to a contextual equation involving one literal. Uniform
          substitution extends that equation to every formula. Two structural isomorphisms of Došen
          and Petrić then force the product projections to coincide. A Lean formalization proves the
          maximality argument relative to explicit coherence and structural inputs. Tagged occurrence
          sets give coordinates for the reduction and substitution.

Mathematics Subject Classification (2020). Primary 03G30; Secondary 03F07, 18A32.
Keywords. Boolean categories, maximality, categorial proof theory, coherence, substitution congru-
ences.


1        Introduction
Došen and Petrić’s Boolean proof category admits distinct arrows with the same source and target.
Their Boolean Coherence theorem distinguishes these arrows by relations between occurrences of
propositional variables. In [1, §14.2, p. 322], they leave open whether the category is maximal
and conjecture that it is not. We prove maximality: adjoining any new arrow equation as a
substitution-closed schema identifies every pair of parallel arrows.
    The proof has two parts. First, a relation distinguishing two arrows contains an edge that can
be isolated by composition with a selector and a coselector. This yields the contextual equation
used in Došen and Petrić’s relative-maximality argument [1, §9.7, pp. 215–216]. Second, we apply
two isomorphisms listed in [1, §12.5, p. 294] to turn that contextual equation into equality of the
product projections. Their invertibility also follows from the proof of [4, Lemma 7]. This application
of the isomorphisms proves the required collapse.
    Došen and Petrić prove maximality for cartesian categories [2, §5]. Their proof uses coherence,
uniform substitution, and precomposition and postcomposition to reduce a new cartesian equation
to equality of the product projections. The corresponding reduction for Boolean arrow equations is
proved in Sections 4 and 5.
    Earlier results establish maximality for lattice categories and relative maximality for dicartesian
categories [3, §§8–10]; these results concern products and coproducts without distribution. Related
occurrence-selection constructions appear in [4, Lemma 6]. A differing-edge argument also proves

2    The Boolean proof category
Fix a nonempty set P of propositional variables. Write Form(P ) for the formulas built from P ,
the constants ⊤, ⊥, the binary connectives ∧, ∨, and negation ¬. Let B(P ) be the freely generated
zero-identity Boolean category defined in [1, §§14.2–14.3]. Its objects are these formulas, and its
arrows are the arrow terms of that presentation modulo its equations. We write B when P is fixed.
We recall the structure and equations used in the proof.
    The connectives ∧ and ∨ give binary products and coproducts; ⊤ is terminal and ⊥ is initial.
For A1 = A and A2 = B, denote the projections and injections by

                     πiA,B : A ∧ B −→ Ai ,       ιA,B
                                                  i   : Ai −→ A ∨ B          (i = 1, 2).

Pairing and copairing are denoted by ⟨f, g⟩ and [f, g], respectively. Write !A : A → ⊤ and κA : ⊥ → A
for the terminal and initial arrows, and 1A for the identity of A. Composition g ◦ f applies f first.
The symbols f ∧ g and f ∨ g denote the arrows induced by the product and coproduct bifunctors.
    The distributor is an arrow

                                  dA,B,C : A ∧ (B ∨ C) −→ (A ∧ B) ∨ C.

Its first-injection equation is

                                                     1   ) = ιA∧B,C

This is the coproduct-injection equation of [1, §11.1, p. 234].
   Negation is represented by a functor ¬ : Bop → B, with specified natural double-negation and
De Morgan isomorphisms. In particular, write nA : ¬¬A → A for the double-negation isomorphism.
The presentation also has arrows ηA : ⊤ → ¬A ∨ A and ϵA : A ∧ ¬A → ⊥. The zero identity is the
endomorphism 0A : A → A defined by

                      0A = [κA , 1A ] ◦ (ϵA ∨ 1A ) ◦ dA,¬A,A ◦ (1A ∧ ηA ) ◦ ⟨1A , !A ⟩.           (2)

Equation (2) is (0ηϵ) of [1, §14.2, p. 317]. Its product and coproduct unit isomorphisms are expressed

Remark. Appendix B gives diagrammatic forms of equations (1)–(3), with the source and target of

    Whenever B(A, C) is nonempty, define 0A,C = f ◦ 0A for any f : A → C. Equation (3) makes
this independent of f : both f ◦ 0A and g ◦ 0A equal 0C ◦ g. Taking f = g gives f ◦ 0A = 0C ◦ f .
Consequently zero arrows absorb composition whenever the composites are defined: h ◦ 0A,C = 0A,D
for h : C → D, and 0A,C ◦ k = 0X,C for k : X → A.
    Two arrows are parallel when they have the same source and target. A proof congruence on B is
an equivalence relation on each hom-set preserved by composition and every arrow operation of the
presentation, including the derived operations of pairing, copairing, and negation. A substitution
congruence is a proof congruence also preserved by every uniform substitution σ : P → Form(P ).
Here substitution acts on the entire arrow term, including its source and target; this is rule (su) of
[1, §2.7, p. 51].
    The total congruence identifies every parallel pair. A category is thin, or a preorder, when each
hom-set has at most one element. We call B maximal when its only substitution congruences are
equality and the total congruence. The main result is the following theorem.

Theorem 2.1. Let P be a nonempty set of propositional variables. The freely generated zero-identity
Boolean category B(P ) of Došen and Petrić is maximal. Equivalently, every substitution congruence
on B(P ) that identifies two distinct parallel arrows identifies all parallel arrows.

    The proof is completed in Section 5.


3    Occurrences and substitution
For a formula A, write Occ(A) for the finite set of positions occupied by propositional variables in
A. Repeated occurrences of the same variable occupy distinct positions. We use tagged coordinates
for these positions.
    For finite sets X, Y , let X ⊔ Y = ({1} × X) ∪ ({2} × Y ) be their tagged disjoint union. The
occurrence sets are defined recursively by

                  Occ(p) = {∗},            Occ(⊤) = Occ(⊥) = ∅,
               Occ(¬A) = Occ(A), Occ(A ⋆ C) = Occ(A) ⊔ Occ(C)              (⋆ ∈ {∧, ∨}).

The label map λA : Occ(A) → P assigns to each occurrence its underlying variable. Distinct
positions may have the same label. The left-to-right order gives a bijection from Occ(A) to
{1, . . . , | Occ(A)|}, interpreted as the empty set when the cardinality is zero. This bijection relates
the tagged coordinates to the finite ordinals used in [1]; Appendix A.2 expresses the corresponding
change of substitution coordinates by a commutative square and a worked example.
    Let Relfin be the category of finite sets and binary relations. A relation R : X → Y is a subset
of X × Y ; identities are diagonal relations, and composition is relational composition. Transporting
the occurrence interpretation of [1] to the tagged coordinates gives a functor G : B → Relfin with
GA = Occ(A). For example,

    Joyal’s collapse theorem concerns cartesian closed categories with a dualizing object; see [1,

                         = B(⊤, p ⇒ p), a contradiction.
    A signed literal is p or ¬p for p ∈ P . A formula is in negation normal form when negation
occurs only immediately before a propositional variable. The double-negation and De Morgan
isomorphisms, including the isomorphisms for the truth constants, give an isomorphism aA : A → A◦
to a formula in negation normal form. Its occurrence relation is the graph of the correspondence of
positions [1, §14.1, pp. 313–315].
    For arrows between formulas in negation normal form, every edge of the occurrence relation joins
occurrences of the same signed literal. Indeed, the equivalent presentation of [1, §14.2, pp. 318–321]
has signed literals as generators. Its positive structural generators preserve these labels; zero
endomorphisms and the arrows ηp and ϵp have empty relations. Composition and both tensor
operations preserve label agreement. The equivalence with B preserves the occurrence interpretation.

5    Contextual collapse
We use the following two isomorphisms to remove the constants from (KA ). Their types occur
among the isomorphisms of [1, §12.5, p. 294]; the proof identifies the particular arrows needed here.
Lemma 5.1. For every formula A, the arrows

                          qA = [jA ◦ π1A,⊥ , ιA,⊤
                                              2 ] : (A ∧ ⊥) ∨ ⊤ −→ A ∨ ⊤,
                          eA = 1A ∧ jA : A ∧ A −→ A ∧ (A ∨ ⊤)
are isomorphisms in B.
Proof. First take A = p ∈ P . By [1, §12.5, p. 294], the source and target of qp are isomorphic. Each
has one occurrence of p, so the relation of any such isomorphism is the unique bijection between
these occurrence sets. The arrow qp has the same relation. Boolean Coherence therefore identifies
qp with that isomorphism.
     The same cited list gives an isomorphism b : p ∧ p → p ∧ (p ∨ ⊤): take n = m = 1 in
pn ∧ (pm ∨ ⊤) ∼ = pn+m and choose p2 = p ∧ p. The relation Gb is a bijection between two-element
occurrence sets. After precomposing b with the product symmetry if necessary, it agrees with
the order-preserving bijection Gep . Coherence then identifies ep with an isomorphism. These two
identifications also follow from the occurrence-bijection construction in the proof of [4, Lemma 7].
     For arbitrary A, substitute A for p and fix the other variables. The functor Fσ sends qp , ep to
qA , eA and preserves their inverse equations.

θ for every formula A. Then B/θ is a preorder.

Proof of Theorem 2.1. Suppose that a substitution congruence θ on B(P ) identifies two distinct

    The same argument gives the formulation of maximality in terms of models used in [1, §9.3,
p. 194]. An interpretation in a zero-identity Boolean category C assigns an object of C to each
propositional variable and interprets the specified operations; it induces a structure-preserving
functor B(P ) → C.
Corollary 5.3. Let P be a nonempty set of propositional variables, and let f, g : A → C be distinct
arrows of B(P ). If a zero-identity Boolean category C satisfies f = g under every interpretation of
the propositional variables, then C is a preorder.
Proof. Declare two parallel arrows of B(P ) equivalent when all their interpretations in C agree. This
defines a substitution congruence: composing an interpretation with Fσ is another interpretation.
The congruence contains f = g, so Theorem 2.1 makes it total. In particular, π1p,p = π2p,p holds under
every interpretation, for any p ∈ P . Interpret p as an arbitrary object X of C. Its two projections
X ∧ X → X are equal. For any arrows a, b : Y → X, composing ⟨a, b⟩ : Y → X ∧ X with the equal
projections gives a = b.


6    Lean formalization
The accompanying Lean 4 development [9] represents formulas and arrow terms by inductive
types. An arrow term is indexed by its source and target formulas. The generated equational
relation Derives defines a quotient category, in which the structural operations and zero-identity
construction are proved to satisfy their specified equations. Formula substitution, normalization
and the tagged occurrence interpretation are constructed on this syntax.
    The theorem relative_maximality formalizes the passage from a new substitution-closed
equation to totality of the congruence. Its arguments include the structural operations, a faithful
occurrence interpretation, normalization, signed-label preservation, substitution and double negation.
The contextual-collapse theorem uses the symmetric map

                                hA = [1A , 1A ] ◦ mA,A : A ∧ A −→ A,

where the mix arrow mA,C : A ∧ C → A ∨ C is given, for any formula B, by

                            mA,C = (π1A,B ∨ 1C ) ◦ dA,B,C ◦ (1A ∧ ιB,C
                                                                   2   ).

                                                  7
This is equation (dm) of [1, §11.1, p. 234]. Its independence of B and the symmetry of mix give
hA ◦ ⟨π2A,A , π1A,A ⟩ = hA . The formal proof uses this identity to derive projection equality from the
contextual equations.
   For the equation-generated Došen–Petrić presentation, the development proves that the occur-
rence interpretation is well defined and preserves signed labels. Faithfulness of this interpretation
remains unproved in Lean, and the full presentation has not yet been supplied as an instance of all
the hypotheses of relative_maximality.


A     Substitution in occurrence coordinates

arguments are substituted into repeated positions. We give its diagrammatic description from [10,
§§9.2–9.3]. The diagrams separate the choice of an outer occurrence, the choice of an occurrence
within an inserted formula, and the name assigned to that occurrence.

A.3    Shared variables and the maximality argument
The local placeholders may be assigned names in a common variable set Q. Let ρ : C → Q be that
assignment; different elements of C may have the same image. Replace each placeholder c ∈ C by the
variable ρ(c), obtaining the renaming map (−)[ρ] : Form(C) → Form(Q). Define σ : U → Form(Q)
by σ(pi ) = N
            ei [ρ]. Then the following square commutes:

                                                  (−)[Φ0 ]

                                    (−)[σ]                        (−)[ρ]                             (11)

                                     Form(Q)         1
                                                             Form(Q).

At pi , both routes give σ(pi ). The constant and connective clauses agree, so structural induction
proves commutativity. Renaming changes the labels on the occurrences, while the pairs (r, t)
continue to distinguish their positions. This permits the same actual variable to occur in several
inserted patterns without identifying their separate occurrences.

and p ∨ ⊤; each has one occurrence of p. Their substituted occurrence sets therefore each contain
one copy of Occ(A), and the substituted arrow has type A ∧ ⊥ → A ∨ ⊤. If the isolated literal is
¬p, the substitution p 7→ ¬A instead gives ¬¬A ∧ ⊥ → ¬¬A ∨ ⊤; the double-negation square in
Proposition 4.2 then gives the required equation for A.

C    Two corrections to the cut-elimination proof
The cut-elimination proof in [1, §11.2] contains two typographical errors in its rank comparisons.
We use the notation of that proof: r is the rank of the cut being reduced and r′ is the rank of a
recursive subcut. The left and right ranks are positive; their sum is the cut rank [1, p. 249].

Page 260, case (w γ 3). In the sentence immediately following the displayed reduction, the
inequality r < r′ should read r′ < r. The recursive cuts have the same cut formula and smaller rank:
the corresponding one-sided ranks decrease on passing to the indicated premises, while the other
one-sided rank is unchanged.

Page 262, case (w ∨ 1). In the continuation of this case, the phrase “whose rank is 1” should
read “whose right rank is 1”, as in the analogous argument on p. 261. The selected formula in the
right premise is newly introduced by the displayed ∨L rule, so its right rank is 1. Its total rank is
the unchanged left rank plus 1, and is therefore at least 2.
